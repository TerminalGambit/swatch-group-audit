# Macro Context — Swatch Group 2024

*Research compiled: May 2026. Sources cited throughout. All figures are 2024 full-year unless otherwise noted.*

---

## 1. China Luxury Slowdown

### Scale of the Contraction

China's mainland luxury market suffered its most severe peacetime contraction in recent history. Bain & Company's 2024 China Luxury Report documented an **18–20% year-on-year decline in domestic luxury spending**, reverting the market back to 2020 levels — effectively erasing four years of post-pandemic recovery in a single year. Total Chinese luxury spending (including overseas purchases) fell approximately **7%**, a smaller figure that masks the severity of the onshore collapse because affluent Chinese consumers redirected spending to international travel destinations rather than abandoning luxury outright.

The Federation of the Swiss Watch Industry (FH) confirmed the direct impact on Swatch Group's core product category: Swiss watch exports to mainland China fell **-25.8% in value in 2024** — a contraction the FH described as "stronger than during the Covid pandemic." Hong Kong SAR, which functions as a key re-export and retail hub for Chinese consumers, saw Swiss watch exports fall a further **-18.7%**. In customs data terms, China's total watch imports fell **-23.6% in volume to 7.6 million units**, with the value dropping to USD 2.9 billion.

### Structural Demand Drivers

**Property wealth destruction.** Residential property constitutes approximately **70% of urban household assets** in China, making its collapse uniquely destructive to consumer confidence. By end-2024, average new home prices had fallen roughly **20% from their 2021 peak**, with unsold residential floor space reaching 381 million square meters — approximately 170% above pre-pandemic norms. Macquarie Group estimated that roughly **85% of the price gains** underlying Chinese household wealth creation since 2010 had evaporated by 2024. The IMF attributed the property downturn with reducing China's annual real GDP growth by approximately **2 percentage points per annum** in 2024 and 2025.

**Youth unemployment.** China's surveyed youth unemployment rate (ages 16–24, excluding students under the revised NBS methodology introduced in 2024) peaked at **18.8% in August 2024** — the highest level recorded under the revised methodology. The prior-methodology peak of 21.3% in June 2023 had prompted the NBS to suspend publication for six months. Sustained double-digit youth unemployment depresses aspirational spending among China's most important future luxury consumer cohort.

**Consumer confidence index.** The NBS Consumer Confidence Index reached its lowest level in 22 months in October 2024, with a minimum reading of 94.19 recorded in August 2024. Bain described affluent consumers becoming "more conservative in their luxury spending" and diversifying wealth "across broader asset classes rather than concentrating on luxury goods."

**"Luxury shame" and anti-ostentation sentiment.** A qualitative but commercially significant trend emerged in 2024 that Bain labeled "luxury shame." As reported by CNBC (July 2024) and corroborated by the South China Morning Post, China's wealthy began deliberately avoiding conspicuous consumption in response to two converging forces: economic anxiety about appearing out of touch with a struggling population, and continued political pressure from the Xi Jinping government's "common prosperity" campaign. The CCP's crackdown on "wealth flaunting" — including banning high-profile influencers known for lavish lifestyles from social media in May 2024 — reinforced this behavioral shift. Watches, as a particularly legible status symbol, were among the hardest-hit categories. This dynamic echoes the 2012–2014 episode when Xi's anti-corruption campaign drove Swiss watch exports to China down more than 25% in a single quarter.

**Overseas spending redistribution.** Chinese luxury spending did not disappear — it migrated. Bain estimated that **40% of total Chinese luxury spending occurred overseas in 2024**, with Asia-Pacific destinations (especially Japan) receiving approximately **120% of their 2019 level** of Chinese luxury spending. Europe recovered to only ~50% of 2019 levels. This redistribution benefited Swatch Group's Japanese operations (record sales, +30% growth) and hurt its mainland China and Hong Kong distribution.

**Hainan duty-free market.** Hainan's duty-free channel, which had been a major onshore luxury access point, suffered a **~29% decline in 2024**, further compressing domestic sales.

### Category-Level Impact on Watches

Among all luxury categories, watches and jewelry faced "the most significant challenges," per Bain's China analysis, as consumers shifted preferences toward "other value-preserving assets and experiences." Declining secondary market prices for luxury watches — driven partly by the global pre-owned market correction — reduced the investment narrative that had driven Chinese watch accumulation during 2021–2022. Bain's global luxury market data confirmed watches as the worst-performing personal luxury goods category in 2024, declining **-5 to -7% globally** to reach €51 billion, versus jewelry (+0–2%) and beauty (+3–5%).

## 2. Hong Kong & Macau

### Hong Kong Retail Collapse

Hong Kong's retail sector experienced a sustained and severe contraction throughout 2024, driven by a structural shift in Chinese tourist spending behavior. Visitors increasingly arrived for experiences (dining, events, free attractions) rather than retail purchases, while the relatively strong Hong Kong dollar (pegged to the USD, itself at multi-year highs against Asian currencies) made Hong Kong goods expensive relative to Japan in particular.

Key monthly data points from the Hong Kong Census and Statistics Department:
- May 2024: jewelry, watches, clocks and valuable gifts **-21.4% year-on-year**
- July 2024: jewelry, watches and valuable gifts **-25% year-on-year**
- November 2024: jewelry, watches and valuable gifts **-5.4% year-on-year** (some moderation)
- January–August 2024 cumulative total retail sales: **-8% year-on-year**

PwC Hong Kong forecast the full-year 2024 retail sales decline at approximately **-6%** (to HKD 383 billion), with the jewelry and watch segment underperforming the broader retail market significantly throughout the first three quarters.

The paradox is that **visitor arrivals actually rose**: the Hong Kong Tourism Board (HKTB) confirmed close to **45 million visitor arrivals in 2024, up 31% year-on-year**, with overnight visitors accounting for half of all arrivals. Inbound visitor spending reached HKD 195 billion (+9.6% versus 2023). However, this spending growth was concentrated in hospitality, food and beverage, and entertainment — not hard luxury retail. Chinese tourists, who previously anchored Hong Kong's watch and jewelry economy, shifted their spending patterns fundamentally.

Swiss watch exports to Hong Kong fell **-18.7%** in 2024, causing Hong Kong to lose its third-place ranking in global Swiss watch export destinations.

### Macau: Gaming Rebound, Retail Divergence

Macau's gaming sector staged a strong recovery: gross gaming revenue (GGR) reached **MOP 226.8 billion (USD 28.1 billion)** in 2024, up **+23.9% year-on-year**, surpassing the government's own target of MOP 216 billion. This represented approximately 77.5% of 2019 GGR levels.

However, the Macau gaming recovery was concentrated in mass-market table games rather than the VIP junket segment that had been a historical driver of high-end retail spending. VIP gross gaming revenues, while recovering, remained well below pre-pandemic levels as junket operators had largely been eliminated following the 2022 crackdown. The tourist profile shifted toward lower-stakes, day-trip visitors who contributed less to hard luxury retail adjacent to casino floors.

Swatch Group's management confirmed that "Southeast Asian markets heavily dependent on Chinese tourists" saw sales decline approximately **-30% overall**, consistent with the data from both Hong Kong and Macau-adjacent channels.

## 3. CHF Currency Dynamics

### The SNB's Unprecedented 2024 Easing Cycle

The Swiss National Bank became the first major developed-market central bank to cut interest rates in the post-pandemic tightening cycle, initiating a sequence of four rate reductions across 2024:

| Meeting | Cut | Rate After |
|---------|-----|-----------|
| March 2024 | -25 bps | 1.50% |
| June 2024 | -25 bps | 1.25% |
| September 2024 | -25 bps | 1.00% |
| December 2024 | -50 bps | 0.50% |

**Total: -125 basis points in 2024** — the most aggressive annual SNB easing since the 2008 financial crisis. The December 50 bps cut was described as "surprising" by Bloomberg, signaling the SNB's concern about CHF strength actively damaging Swiss export competitiveness.

### Exchange Rate Movements in 2024

The CHF's behavior in 2024 was complex and ultimately unhelpful for Swiss watch manufacturers:

**CHF vs CNY:** The average 2024 CHF/CNY rate was **8.1586**, compared to approximately 8.56 in 2023 — representing a **~4.4% CHF depreciation against the yuan** over the year. This was theoretically mildly positive for Swiss watch price competitiveness in China, but was overwhelmed by demand destruction.

**CHF vs USD:** The USD appreciated approximately **+7.8% against the CHF** in 2024, meaning Swiss goods became cheaper for American buyers — directly supporting Swatch Group's record US performance.

**CHF vs EUR:** EUR/CHF was approximately flat, with the Swiss franc weakening only ~1.1% against the euro.

**Net effect for Swatch Group:** Despite the SNB's aggressive cutting, the CHF remained strong in absolute terms — particularly against Asian currencies (JPY, KRW, CNY) where imported luxury markets were booming precisely because of currency weakness. The company explicitly cited a **-2.4% translation drag from currency effects**, amounting to approximately **CHF 192 million** in lost reported revenue versus constant-currency performance. At constant exchange rates, the group's sales decline was -12.2%; at actual exchange rates reported, it was -14.6% — confirming the ~2.4 percentage point currency headwind.

The SNB's rate cuts represent a structural acknowledgment that CHF strength has become a threat to Swiss industry competitiveness, particularly watch exports. The rate trajectory from 1.75% (start of 2024) to 0.5% (end of 2024) signals sustained policy accommodation, though the SNB cannot depreciate the franc without significant intervention.

## 4. Global Luxury Market 2024

### A Landmark Year of Contraction

The Bain-Altagamma 2024 Worldwide Luxury Market Monitor confirmed that 2024 was the personal luxury goods market's **first contraction in 15 years (excluding the Covid-19 shock of 2020)**. The market fell to **€363 billion**, down **-2% at current exchange rates** from €370 billion in 2023. Total luxury spending across all categories (including experiences, hospitality, cars, and art) reached approximately €1.48 trillion, broadly flat to slightly down.

A particularly diagnostic statistic: **only approximately one-third of luxury brands experienced growth in 2024**, versus 95% of brands growing during 2021–2022 (the post-lockdown boom) and 65% growing in 2023. The luxury consumer base had shrunk by approximately **50 million people over 2023–2024**, reversing the aspirational-consumer expansion that drove the pandemic-era boom.

### Regional Performance Divergence

The global picture in 2024 was one of sharp regional divergence rather than uniform decline:

| Region | 2024 Market Size | Growth |
|--------|-----------------|--------|
| Europe | €110 billion | +3–4% |
| Americas | €100 billion | -1% |
| Japan | €33 billion | +12–13% |
| Mainland China | est. ~€40 billion | -20–22% |
| Rest of Asia-Pacific | n/a | -1–2% |

**Japan** was the standout performer globally, driven by the weakest yen in decades. At its weakest point (July 10, 2024), 1 USD = 161.5 JPY, making Japanese retail prices 20–30% cheaper than equivalent goods in China, Europe, or the Americas. This turbo-charged inbound tourism luxury spending: Japan received a record **36.87 million inbound tourists in 2024 (+47.1% year-on-year)**, with tourist expenditures reaching ¥8 trillion (approximately USD 54.7 billion). The imported luxury brand retail market in Japan rose **+19.7%** in 2024. Swiss watch exports to Japan grew **+7.8%** for the year. Swatch Group specifically achieved **record sales in Japan with over 30% growth**, as Harry Winston, Omega, Longines, and Tissot all gained share.

**Americas** showed resilience: Swiss watch exports to the US grew **+5.0%**, and the US cemented its position as the world's largest single-country Swiss watch export market, overtaking China. Swatch Group confirmed record sales in the USA, with Tissot exceeding USD 100 million in US sales for the first time.

**Europe** was modestly positive, supported by continued inbound tourism from North American and Asian visitors.

**Mainland China** fell from the No. 2 to the No. 4 luxury market globally in 2024, as its decline pushed it behind Japan and broader Asia ex-China ex-Japan.

### Watch Category Specifically

Watches were the worst-performing personal luxury goods category in 2024:
- **Watches: -5 to -7% decline** to approximately **€51 billion**
- Leather goods: -3–5% 
- Shoes: -5–7%
- Jewelry: +0–2% (resilient by comparison)
- Beauty: +3–5% (outperformer)
- Eyewear: +3–5%

The watch category's underperformance relative to jewelry reflects both the severity of the Chinese demand collapse (China was disproportionately large in watches vs. other categories) and the rising appeal of pre-owned watches as substitutes for new purchases.

## 5. Paris Olympics Impact

### Omega's Role and Brand Exposure

Omega served as Official Timekeeper of the Paris 2024 Olympics for the **31st time since 1932**, covering all 329 Olympic events across 32 sports. The operation deployed **550 on-site professionals, 900 trained volunteers, and 350 tons of equipment** including 85 public scoreboards and 350 sports scoreboards connected by 200 km of cable and optical fiber. Omega introduced the Scan'O'Vision ULTIMATE photofinish camera (up to 40,000 images per second).

Brand visibility was high: Omega's red wordmark appeared throughout Paris venues and global broadcasts. Gold medalist sprinter Noah Lyles wore an Omega Speedmaster during the men's 100-meter sprint; pole vaulter Armand Duplantis wore an Omega while setting a world record. Both Omega and Swatch launched special Paris 2024 edition collections.

### The Marketing Paradox

Despite this high-profile Olympic presence, empirical data contradicts any near-term commercial benefit. Fortune (September 2024) reported that used Omega watch prices declined by **nearly 1%** in the 30-day period following the Games, per the Bloomberg Subdial Watch Index — while Rolex, Patek Philippe, Audemars Piguet, Cartier, and Tudor prices rose over the same period. The broader used watch market rose less than 1% in the same window.

This suggests that Olympic sponsorship, while generating brand awareness, did not translate into demand recovery in 2024. The sponsorship's strategic value is long-term brand equity and association with precision/performance — not a near-term sales catalyst.

The financial burden is also significant, though Swatch Group does not disclose specific sponsorship figures. The Omega–IOC Worldwide Olympic Partnership extends through the **Brisbane 2032 Games**, representing a multi-decade financial commitment. Industry analysts estimate Olympic timekeeping contracts of this scope involve annual commitments in the hundreds of millions of CHF when factoring in rights fees, operational logistics, athlete sponsorships, and media campaigns. Swatch Group management cited Olympic marketing spend as one of three explicit factors pressuring the operating margin in 2024. The operating margin collapsed from 15.1% to 4.5% — a 74.5% decline in absolute operating profit — partly reflecting this elevated non-recurring marketing burden during an Olympic year.

## 6. Structural Threats

### Smartwatch Market Dynamics

The traditional vs. smart watch debate often overstates competitive threat to the high-end segment, but the 2024 data presents a nuanced picture. Counterpoint Research reported that global smartwatch shipments **declined for the first time ever in 2024, falling -7% in volume**. Apple Watch specifically fell -19%, with Apple's market share dropping from 25% to 22%. Samsung shipments rose +3%, while Xiaomi surged +135% on aggressive pricing.

The smartwatch market's own decline suggests the competitive pressure on traditional watchmakers from smartwatches may be easing at the premium tier. However, at Swatch Group's mid-range price points (Tissot, Swatch brand: CHF 100–1,000), smartwatches from Samsung and Chinese manufacturers (Huawei, Xiaomi) offering health monitoring, connectivity, and comparable build quality at CHF 200–400 remain a direct substitute for entry-level mechanical and quartz watches.

Swatch Group's strategic response — including the SPOT smartwatch under the Swatch brand and various connected watch initiatives — has not achieved the commercial traction of competitors, leaving its mid-market brands exposed.

### Pre-Owned and Secondary Market Growth

The secondary watch market is a structural disruptor that simultaneously competes with and validates new watch sales:

- Global pre-owned luxury watch market was valued at approximately **USD 26.8–28.8 billion in 2024**, growing approximately **+10% year-on-year**
- The secondary market is projected to grow to **USD 43.7 billion by 2031** (CAGR ~11.6%)
- Deloitte's Swiss Watch Industry Insights 2024 projected the pre-owned market will be **as large as the primary market within 10 years**
- **38% of Gen Z** respondents indicated they were likely to purchase a pre-owned watch in the next 12 months (versus 17% of Baby Boomers), driven by value-for-money, sustainability, and access to discontinued references (Deloitte)

The growth of platforms including Chrono24, WatchBox, Watchfinder, and brand-certified resale programs (Omega itself entered the certified pre-owned space) creates a structural headwind for new watch sales, particularly in Swatch Group's mid-to-upper price tiers. Consumers who previously aspired to a new Longines or Omega can now access a pre-owned example at 30–50% below retail through authenticated platforms, compressing primary market demand.

Critically, the secondary market's price normalization in 2024 — after the pandemic-era spike in pre-owned watch prices — may have suppressed new watch demand by reducing the "investment premium" narrative that Chinese buyers in particular had used to justify luxury watch accumulation.

### Generational Shift and Brand Relevance

Bain's 2024 global luxury report identified a structural demand risk: the luxury customer base had **shrunk by approximately 50 million people over 2023–2024**, with Gen Z "advocacy for luxury brands continuing to decline." Gen Z's preferences trend toward:
- Experiences over objects
- Sustainability credentials (55% of Gen Z watch buyers prefer online channels with transparent supply chains, per Deloitte)
- Secondhand and neo-vintage references over new luxury
- Casualization of dress codes reducing the social occasions requiring a formal watch

This generational preference shift represents a medium-term structural challenge for Swatch Group's higher-end brands (Omega, Longines, Rado), though the Swatch brand's pop-culture collaborations and limited editions have demonstrated effectiveness at engaging younger consumers.

## Key Audit Takeaway

Swatch Group's 2024 performance — net sales down -14.6%, operating margin collapsing from 15.1% to 4.5%, and absolute operating profit falling -74.5% — reflects the company's extraordinarily concentrated exposure to a single macro shock: the Chinese luxury demand collapse. The group's China, Hong Kong, and Macau region, which represented 33% of net sales in 2023, contracted by approximately 30% in 2024. The Swiss watch industry as a whole saw Chinese exports fall 25.8% — described by the FH as worse than the Covid pandemic's demand shock — confirming that Swatch Group's China pain was sector-wide rather than company-specific. The group's mid-market price weighting (Omega, Longines, Tissot, Swatch) made it more vulnerable than peers like Rolex or Patek Philippe, whose ultra-high-net-worth clientele is less sensitive to consumer confidence and property wealth cycles.

The three factors cited by management — China slowdown, CHF currency headwind, and Olympic marketing spend — are real and verifiable against external data, but they are not equally significant. China demand destruction was the dominant driver, accounting for the majority of the revenue shortfall and most of the margin collapse through operating leverage on a high fixed-cost manufacturing base. Currency effects (-2.4% on sales, CHF ~192 million) were material but secondary. The Olympic marketing spend was likely a CHF 100–300 million+ investment that was strategically defensible (the Omega–IOC partnership runs through 2032) but created an acute timing mismatch: peak expenditure coincided with the weakest revenue environment in over a decade. Structural threats — smartwatch competition, pre-owned market growth, Gen Z brand drift — do not explain the 2024 shortfall but create the underlying context for why recovery may be slower than management's current guidance implies, particularly if China's property market and consumer confidence do not recover materially in 2025.
